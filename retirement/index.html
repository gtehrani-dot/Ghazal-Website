Methodology

The Three Funding Sources

Every dollar in this IRA traces back to one of three 401(k) rollovers — no direct contributions were ever made to the IRA itself

Decision Lens (DL) 401(k) 5/14/2014 $64,526.20 total, of which $19,960.30 was already in the account as of 4/30/2011. Split: the $19,960.30 premarital principal plus its own proportional share of DL's real 2011–2014 growth ($23,967.13) is premarital; the $33,778.39 of marital contributions plus their share of that same growth ($40,559.07) is marital.
FranConnect 401(k) 11/13/2024 $55,906.37. 100% marital — employment began after the 2011 marriage; entire balance accrued during the marriage.
Why BAH dropped from $61,103.74 (2008) to $48,537.40 (2011) before growing to $82,071.29 (2014): the 2008 ING statement shows the rollover was converted to a Roth IRA the same month, with 10% federal tax withheld ($6,114.41) — only $55,029.65 actually reached the Roth. It was later recharacterized back to a Traditional IRA, and the remaining balance was invested through the 2008–2009 financial crisis before partially recovering. This is real, statement-documented history, not a data error — and it doesn't inflate the analysis, since the model uses the actual, lower 2011 value as its marriage-date starting point, not the original 2008 rollover amount.

Why actual account performance, not the S&P 500

Applying an S&P 500 return to the premarital portion would overstate today's marital estate, because this account's actual, documented performance lagged the market — including a multi-year stretch (2015–2020) in illiquid real estate investments and cash at PENSCO that earned little to no return. Using a market index as a stand-in for growth only makes sense when the real numbers aren't available; here, they are.

Because the BAH, DL, and FranConnect rollovers were pooled into the same investment lineup once inside the IRA, every dollar in the account earned the identical percentage gain or loss during any period they were together. That's what makes a pro-rata (“coverture fraction”) tracing approach both mathematically sound and easy to verify against the statements: once a premarital/marital ownership percentage is established at the moment funds combine, that same percentage carries forward automatically as the whole account rises or falls.

Open items

Assumptions to Confirm with Counsel / Mediator Before Finalizing

  • Valuation date: this proposal uses the 6/30/2026 Fidelity statement as a placeholder. It should be re-run using whichever date is the controlling valuation date (date of separation, date of filing, or another negotiated date) — the model updates cleanly to any date with an available statement.
  • DL 401(k) growth, 2011–2014: rather than treating DL's entire increase in value as marital, this proposal traces DL's own real growth ($10,787.51, net of fees) proportionally between its premarital principal and its marital contributions, based on their actual share of DL's basis (37.14% / 62.86%) — the same pro-rata tracing approach used everywhere else in this proposal. A more granular alternative would match each individual paycheck contribution to the fund performance from its specific contribution date forward, rather than using one blended proportion for the whole 2011–2014 period; given all contributions sat in the same pooled holdings throughout, the practical difference is likely small, but your attorney may want that finer cut if precision here is contested.
  • Treatment of passive appreciation on separate property: Virginia follows the source-of-funds rule (Va. Code § 20-107.3), under which passive, market-driven growth on separate property stays separate even when it occurs during the marriage. That's the rule this proposal applies to BAH's growth. It's worth confirming with your attorney that a court would characterize BAH's growth as passive (no active management by either spouse) rather than active — that's the one fact question underlying this legal conclusion. Separately, Va. Code § 20-107.3(G) caps what a non-owning spouse may be awarded from the marital share of a retirement account at 50% of that share.
  • The Fidelity→PENSCO (2015) and PENSCO→Fidelity (2020) transfers were treated as in-kind/cash movements of the same IRA assets, not new contributions or distributions — consistent with the statements, which show no tax withholding or distribution coding on these transfers.

Virginia's rule on growth: under Va. Code § 20-107.3, Virginia applies the source-of-funds rule — retirement value earned before marriage is separate property, and passive (market-driven) appreciation on that separate share stays separate even when it occurs during the marriage. BAH sat untouched with no active management by either spouse, so its full documented growth — including the 2011–2014 stretch, before it merged with DL — stays premarital. This is why Step 1 uses BAH's actual value at the moment of merger ($82,071.29, April 2014) rather than its lower value at the 2011 marriage date ($48,537.40): both figures represent the same 100%-premarital money, just measured at different points in its real, documented history.
